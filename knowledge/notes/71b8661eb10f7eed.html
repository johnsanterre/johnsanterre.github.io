I Bought a CO2 Monitor, and It Broke Me

indoor-air-qualitycarbon-dioxidegas-stovesventilationaranet4

Abstraction: Personal experience revealing the limits and anxiety of CO2 home monitoring

Key points:

  • Author's Boston apartment registered 1,200 ppm CO2 at baseline; user manual cited 15% cognitive function reduction at that level and 50% reduction above 1,400 ppm; outdoor baseline was 480 ppm
  • The Aranet4 ($250) is the brand most indoor-air experts recommend; atmospheric chemist Jose-Luis Jimenez (U. Colorado Boulder) found it competitive with $20,000 research-grade sensors
  • CO2 is a proxy for "staleness" of air and other pollutants but is not itself the worst hazard; nitrogen oxide, carbon monoxide, ozone, and particulate matter cause more direct harm; Aranet4 does not measure PM
  • Gas stove ignition briefly spiked readings to 2,000+ ppm; practical remedies (open windows, range fans) proved insufficient in small, poorly ventilated apartments without outside-venting hoods
  • Experts note the most sustainable solutions—induction stoves, HVAC upgrades, energy-efficient ventilation—require home ownership and significant capital, leaving renters largely unable to fix the problem
  • The broader lesson: a measurement device that reveals a problem you cannot solve amplifies anxiety rather than enabling control; experts suggest "use the tools you have and don't make yourself crazy"
