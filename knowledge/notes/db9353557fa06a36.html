Where Are the Last of Maine's Historic King Pines?

historyold-growth-forestcolonial-historyecology

Abstraction: History and survival of British-claimed king pine trees in Maine forests

Key points:

  • British surveyors marked eastern white pines over 24 inches diameter with a "broad arrow" (three hatchet blows) for exclusive use as Royal Navy masts; cutting them carried heavy penalties
  • Some trees reached 40 inches diameter, far exceeding Baltic mast sources (max ~27 inches); the king's claim fomented colonial resentment including the 1772 Pine Tree Riot in Weare, NH — a precursor to the Revolution
  • The Hermitage preserve in Maine is a rare old-growth stand (~120-foot pines); only 1% of Maine's forests are considered old, and a fraction of that has never been harvested
  • Finding surviving broad arrow scars is nearly impossible — tree bark grows over wounds over centuries, and most of Maine was logged repeatedly in the 19th–20th centuries
  • One confirmed king pine remnant (with visible broad arrow scar) is displayed in a gazebo in Ashland, Maine after a 1960s forester salvaged it from Aroostook County
  • Old-growth forests provide irreplaceable ecological value: rare lichens, deep-burrowing beetles, carbon sequestration — with near-incalculable monetary worth
