Yes, I'm Autistic. No, I'm Not a STEM Savant

autismneurodiversityworkplace-inclusionstem-stereotypesdisability

Abstraction: First-person account challenging STEM savant stereotype of autistic workers

Key points:

  • The "autistic STEM genius" stereotype harms autistic workers regardless of their actual skills by pressuring them to find a "super skill" rather than requiring employers to be more accepting
  • Autistic people are employed across all fields — arts, policy, retail, communications — not just STEM; autism exists across all genders and ethnicities, but diagnosis disparities disadvantage women and people of color
  • "Masking" (mimicking neurotypical behavior) is exhausting and unsustainable; accommodations like calendar-blocked focus time, direct communication, and camera-off options in video calls can make work accessible
  • "Functioning labels" (high/low functioning) do more harm than good; autism is better modeled as a spider graph of varied abilities than a linear scale
  • Simple workplace supports — more direct communication, tolerance for non-eye-contact, flexibility in task sequencing — can make major differences with minimal cost
  • Autistic advocacy journalist Eric Garcia: "These narratives put the onus on autistic people to find a super skill rather than forcing employers to become more accepting"
